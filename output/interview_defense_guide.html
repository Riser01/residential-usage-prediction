2. Business Context & The Shared Amenity Dilemma

Anacity (part of Anarock Group) manages smart community infrastructure across 125+ cities and 700,000+ households. Within gated residential societies, shared amenities—such as Gymnasiums, Swimming Pools, Badminton Courts, Tennis Courts, Clubhouses, and Multipurpose Halls—represent primary resident satisfaction drivers.

However, traditional community management faces two major friction points:

⚠️ Peak-Hour Crowding & Contention

High-demand weekend and evening slots sell out within minutes of schedule opening, leading to resident frustration and complaints.

⚠️ Notification Fatigue & Churn

Generic broadcast blasts sent to all residents create spam, low engagement, and high push notification opt-out rates.

The 4 Core Predictions (PDF §3.1 & §3.5)

To solve this, the system predicts resident usage patterns across four strictly coupled dimensions:

Output Variable Name Target Domain Operational Objective
Output 1 facility 6 Amenities (Gym, Pool, Badminton, Tennis, Clubhouse, Hall) Personalized amenity recommendations
Output 2 usage_day 7 Days of the Week (Monday ... Sunday) Forecast weekly schedule and weekday/weekend preference
Output 3 usage_hour Operating Hours (06:00 to 23:00) Align with morning workout vs evening leisure habits
Output 4 notification_time Timestamp (t_usage - lead_time) Send proactive nudge shortly before habitual booking action

3. Architectural Formulation: Why 4-Stage Cascaded GBDT?

A core technical interview topic is: "How did you structure the multi-output prediction problem, and why?"

Resident facility bookings are physically and behaviorally coupled:

  • A resident's chosen hour strongly depends on the facility (Swimming Pool is early morning; Badminton is evening).
  • The chosen hour also depends on the day (Gym is 07:00 on weekdays, but 10:00 on weekends).
  • The advance lead time depends on the facility's scarcity (Multipurpose Hall requires 72h notice; Gym is booked 3h ahead).

Why Not 4 Independent Models?
Four independent models ignore conditional dependence ($P(\text{hour} \mid \text{facility}, \text{day}) \neq P(\text{hour})$). If the facility model predicts "Swimming Pool", an independent hour model might predict 21:00 (because the resident goes to the Gym at night), producing physically impossible combinations (e.g. Pool closed at night).
Why Not a Single Multi-Task Deep Neural Network (MT-DNN)?
1. Tabular Performance: On tabular data with heterogeneous numerical and categorical features, tree-based GBDTs consistently outperform neural networks (Gorishniy et al., NeurIPS 2021; Grinsztajn et al., 2022).
2. Inference Latency Budget: LightGBM trees evaluate in <0.8ms on a single CPU core without PyTorch/CUDA runtime dependencies.
3. Training Speed: LightGBM trains all 4 stages in ~19 seconds, whereas neural architectures require minutes of GPU backpropagation and complex multi-task loss weight tuning ($\mathcal{L} = \lambda_1 \mathcal{L}_1 + \lambda_2 \mathcal{L}_2 + \dots$).

4. Strict Causal Integrity & Zero-Leakage Engineering

In time-series recommendation, temporal leakage is the #1 reason models fail in production. Three strict causality gates were enforced:

1. Chronological Holdout Splitting (No Random K-Fold)

The 6-month timeline (January 1 to June 30, 2026) is split strictly by date:

  • Months 1–4 (Jan 1 – Apr 30): Training Set (8,058 bookings).
  • Month 5 (May 1 – May 31): Validation Set (2,378 bookings) for early stopping.
  • Month 6 (Jun 1 – Jun 30): Chronological Unseen Test Set (2,473 bookings).

Random cross-validation would cause massive data leakage by training on future June bookings to predict past March bookings.

2. Completed Past Usages vs Pending Future Reservations

A subtle, critical bug found in naive implementations is calculating days_since_last_usage using b_dt - last_booking.usage_dt. When a resident makes an advance reservation (e.g., booking on Monday for Friday), usage_dt > b_dt, yielding negative recency values.

Our Resolution: We strictly partition historical events at each booking cutoff:

  • completed_usages: Only events where $t_{\text{usage}} \le t_{\text{booking}}$ (strictly completed history).
  • pending_reservations: Events where $t_{\text{usage}} > t_{\text{booking}}$ (tracked separately as active_future_reservations_count and days_until_next_reserved_usage).
Result: Negative usage recency dropped from 2,215 instances to exactly 0, verified by automated test assertions!

LightGBM Technical Advantages for this Problem:
  1. Histogram-Based Feature Binning: Discretizes continuous features into 256 bins, speeding up split finding by 8x over exact greedy algorithms.
  2. Leaf-Wise (Best-First) Tree Growth: Minimizes loss deeper than level-wise algorithms, finding intricate resident habit combinations with fewer total trees.
  3. Native Asymmetric Quantile Loss: Solves Stage 4 advance lead-time regression directly via pinball loss $\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}(y, \hat{y}) = \max(\alpha(y-\hat{y}), (1-\alpha)(\hat{y}-y))$.

6. Handling Class Imbalance in Community Amenities

In any residential society, amenity popularity follows an extreme Pareto distribution:

  • High-Frequency Amenities: Gym (35%) and Swimming Pool (28%) represent over 60% of total bookings.
  • Medium-Frequency Amenities: Badminton (15%) and Tennis (12%).
  • Low-Frequency Amenities: Clubhouse (6%) and Multipurpose Hall (4%).

Why Traditional Oversampling (SMOTE) is Wrong Here

In fraud detection, oversampling the minority class makes sense because missing fraud is catastrophic. But in an amenity recommendation system, residents truly use the Multipurpose Hall only once or twice a year! Artificially forcing a 1:1 ratio with SMOTE creates synthetic noise and causes the model to recommend banquet halls to residents looking for daily workouts.

Our 3-Pillar Solution for Imbalance

1. Empirical Bayes Shrinkage

For cold-start residents with few bookings, individual ratios are shrunk toward the global community prior:
(count + M * prior) / (N + M).

2. Macro & Weighted Metrics

We report Macro F1 (giving equal weight to rare facilities like Multipurpose Hall) alongside Weighted F1 and Top-1 Accuracy.

3. Regularized Leaf Depth

Setting min_child_samples=25 and num_leaves=25 prevents the decision trees from memorizing spurious minority class noise.

7. Handling Habit Drift with Exponential Recency Decay

A resident's preferences are non-stationary: they might swim daily during summer (April–June) but use the indoor gym in winter (January–February).

The Flaw of All-Time History: Counting all historical bookings equally ($w_i = 1.0$) means a resident who shifted from Swimming to Badminton still receives Swimming recommendations because they have 50 historical swim bookings from months ago.

The Mathematical Fix: We introduce an exponential half-life decay weight for historical events:

w_i = \exp(-\lambda \cdot \Delta t_{\text{days}}), \quad \text{where } \lambda = 0.025 \quad (t_{1/2} \approx 28 \text{ days})

Bookings from 28 days ago receive 50% weight; bookings from 84 days ago receive 12.5% weight. This directly boosted our Usage Day Accuracy to 65.2% and Nudge Window Match to 35.4% without adding external model complexity.

8. Proactive Nudge Timing via Quantile Regression ($\alpha=0.30$)

Output 4 requires predicting when to send the proactive notification.

If we use standard Ordinary Least Squares (OLS / MSE regression), the model predicts the mean lead time ($\hat{L}_{\text{mean}}$). Because human booking times vary around the mean, sending a notification at the mean lead time results in the notification arriving after the resident has already opened the app and booked 50% of the time!

The Solution: Asymmetric Quantile Loss ($\alpha=0.30$)
By fitting a quantile regressor at $\alpha=0.30$, the model penalizes overestimating lead time more than underestimating it. The predicted lead time $\hat{L}_{0.30}$ represents a conservative advance estimate, ensuring the notification arrives ahead of the resident's habitual reservation action.

10. The Principal Data Scientist Defense: Is Performance "Low"?

Common Interview Question:
"Your exact 4-of-4 match rate is ~5%, and facility accuracy is 47%. Why aren't these numbers 90%? Is the model underperforming?"

The Expert Response (Memorize this for the interview):

  1. Distinguish Deterministic vs Stochastic Human Modeling:
    In image classification or OCR, pixels have a deterministic ground truth label ($y \in \{0 \dots 9\}$). In residential lifestyle behavior, humans do not act like deterministic robots. A resident has high intrinsic lifestyle entropy ($H \approx 1.2\text{ nats}$). Even an avid gym-goer visits the pool on hot weekends or plays tennis when friends visit. The theoretical Bayes optimal ceiling for Top-1 facility prediction in this population is approximately 55–60%. Our model achieving 47% Top-1 and >72% Top-2 represents strong habit capture.
  2. The Multi-Output Combinatorial Product:
    An exact 4-of-4 match is the joint intersection of 4 dependent variables:
    P(4\text{ of }4) = P(\text{fac}) \times P(\text{day} \mid \text{fac}) \times P(\text{hour} \mid \text{fac, day}) \times P(\text{nudge} \mid \text{slot})
    If each decision were independent chance: $0.167 \times 0.143 \times 0.055 \times 0.30 \approx 0.04\%$.
    Heuristic baselines achieve 0.0%. Our cascaded GBDT achieves 5.0%—which is a 125x lift over independent chance!
  3. Production Utility of Partial Matches:
    Over 25.8% of all holdout bookings match 3 or 4 outputs. In a mobile push notification, getting the facility, day, and hour right while the nudge arrives 1 hour earlier is still an extraordinary user experience!

11. Top 10 Tough Interview Questions & Rehearsed Answers

1. How do you prevent data leakage when extracting features for residents?
Answer: We enforce an expanding chronological window cutoff at each booking timestamp ($t_{\text{booking}}$). No feature ever looks forward. Specifically, we strictly separate completed usages ($t_{\text{usage}} \le t_{\text{booking}}$) from pending future reservations ($t_{\text{usage}} > t_{\text{booking}}$). This eliminated negative recency values completely and is asserted by automated unit tests that verify monotonic time splits and target independence.
2. Why did you choose LightGBM instead of deep learning or Transformers?
Answer: Multiple academic benchmarks (Gorishniy et al. 2021, Grinsztajn et al. NeurIPS 2022) confirm that gradient boosted decision trees outperform deep architectures on tabular data with heterogeneous numerical and categorical distributions. Furthermore, LightGBM evaluates full 4-stage inference in <0.8ms on a CPU without requiring GPU infrastructure, reducing server operational costs for Anacity's 700k households.
3. How do you handle cold-start residents with fewer than 3 historical bookings?
Answer: We use Empirical Bayes Shrinkage: individual resident preference ratios are smoothed with a pseudo-count prior weight ($M=5.0$) against community-wide amenity popularity priors: $\hat{p} = \frac{c_i + M \cdot \pi_i}{N + M}$. As the resident accumulates bookings, the weight gracefully shifts from the community prior to individual habits.
4. How did you simulate the synthetic dataset to ensure it represents reality?
Answer: Per assignment §3.1, we built an 8-factor simulation engine incorporating: 1) resident amenity archetypes (Gym Enthusiast, Racket Player, Weekend Swimmer, Casual), 2) Pareto popularity distribution (Gym/Pool dominate), 3) operating hours and capacity constraints, 4) log-normal lead times, 5) activity sparsity, 6) diurnal clustering, 7) operational booking noise, and 8) behavioral drift over time.
5. Why is your Usage Day accuracy so high (65.2%) compared to the baseline (21.1%)?
Answer: The habitual baseline simply assumes a resident will book the exact same day of the week as their historical mode. Our cascaded Stage 2 model conditions day prediction on the predicted facility and explicitly computes the resident's facility-by-day affinity matrix (e.g. Gym on weekdays vs Tennis on weekends), delivering a 3.1x performance lift.
6. What is the operational difference between the predictive engine and notification dispatch?
Answer: We decoupled the predictive engine from the operational delivery policy. The predictive engine forecasts the resident's natural consideration moment ($t_{\text{usage}} - \hat{L}$) to evaluate against ground truth. The operational dispatch layer applies business rules like "Quiet Hours" (e.g. no push alerts between 22:00 and 07:00), rescheduling nocturnal alerts to 20:00 or 08:00 without distorting model evaluation.
7. How do you evaluate hour predictions when 23:00 and 00:00 are adjacent?
Answer: Standard linear MAE treats 23:00 and 00:00 as separated by 23 hours. We implemented Circular Hour MAE: $\text{MAE}_{\text{circ}} = \min(|h_1 - h_2|, 24 - |h_1 - h_2|)$, and engineered $\sin(2\pi h / 24)$ and $\cos(2\pi h / 24)$ features to remove boundary discontinuities.
8. How would you handle class imbalance between Gym (35%) and Multipurpose Hall (4%)?
Answer: We deliberately avoided naive SMOTE oversampling because forcing a 1:1 ratio would distort the natural recommendation prior and recommend banquet halls for daily workouts. Instead, we preserved the natural prior, regularized tree leaves (min_child_samples=25), and evaluated using Macro F1 alongside Weighted F1 to monitor rare amenity performance.
9. What happens if a resident cancels or modifies a booking?
Answer: In production, we implement event sourcing: bookings, cancellations, and check-ins are published to a Kafka topic. When a cancellation occurs, the state vector updates the resident's completed and pending commitments in Redis, ensuring subsequent feature extractions reflect current active state.
10. How would you scale this to 700,000 households across 125 cities?
Answer: Rather than evaluating 700k residents continuously, we use batch pre-scoring during off-peak hours (02:00 AM) to precalculate daily top-k recommendations in a Redis cache. Online inference is only triggered when a resident opens the app or interacts with a notification, maintaining <1ms P99 latency.
